Before No. 2, I was a designer who wrote specs. I knew what a good interface
looked like, I knew how to argue for one, and I knew how to hand it off. What
I did not know was what happened after the handoff.

Two weeks of building an app end to end changed that, and not in the way I
expected. The design was not the hard part. The hard part was everything I
had been quietly waving my hand at for years.

## The spec I did not understand I was writing

The first time I wrote "users should only see their own logs," I thought I had
written a requirement. I had actually written a database policy, a session
strategy, and an entire class of bugs I did not know existed yet.

Row-level security is not a detail an engineer adds at the end. It shapes how
the data is modeled from the first table. I had been putting that sentence in
specs for a year without knowing it meant anything.

## Cost is a design constraint

The original plan ran every log through an AI model. It felt obviously
correct: better insights, less work for the user, the kind of thing that demos
well.

Then I did the arithmetic. At a third of a cent per call, once a day, ten
thousand users comes out to about eleven thousand dollars a year — before a
single dollar of revenue exists to pay for it.

So I built a rule-based engine that runs on the device instead. It costs
nothing, it works offline, and the health data never leaves the phone. AI moved
to a phase-two premium feature, where someone is actually paying for it.

That was not a technical compromise. It was a better product decision, and I
only found it because I was close enough to the implementation to see the bill.

## What I would tell my earlier self

Learning the backend did not make me a worse designer or turn me into a
mediocre engineer. It made my specs concrete. When I ask for something now, I
know roughly what it costs, where it breaks, and what I am really trading away.

The gap between "almost right" and "actually right" is where most of the hours
went. It is also the only part anyone notices.
